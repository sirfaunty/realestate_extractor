Module 1: The Language

Core vocabulary — the words you need before anything else makes sense.

The Revenue Stack

Every multifamily property generates revenue in layers. Understanding these layers is how you read a property's financial health at a glance.

GPR — Gross Potential Rent
Total rent if every unit were leased at market rate with zero vacancy. The theoretical maximum.
200 units × $1,500/mo = $300,000 GPR/mo
Vacancy Loss
Revenue lost from unoccupied units. Typically 3-8% of GPR for stabilized properties.
5% vacancy → $300K × 0.05 = $15K/mo lost
Concessions
Discounts given to attract tenants — free months, reduced rent, waived fees. Reduces effective rent below face rent.
"1 month free on a 13-month lease" = ~7.7% concession
EGI — Effective Gross Income
GPR minus vacancy and concessions, plus other income (parking, laundry, pet fees, late fees).
$300K - $15K - $5K + $8K = $288K EGI

The Expense Side

OpEx — Operating Expenses
Day-to-day costs to run the property: payroll, utilities, insurance, property taxes, maintenance, management fees. Does NOT include debt service or capital improvements.
CapEx — Capital Expenditures
Big-ticket improvements that add value or extend useful life: new roof, HVAC replacement, unit renovations. Treated differently from OpEx in valuations.
NOI — Net Operating Income
The single most important number in CRE. Revenue minus operating expenses. The income the property generates before debt service.
$288K EGI - $130K OpEx = $158K NOI/mo
Expense Ratio
OpEx as a percentage of EGI. Typical multifamily: 40-55%. Lower = more efficient.
$130K / $288K = 45.1% expense ratio
NOI = EGI − Operating Expenses

This is the formula you'll hear a hundred times. NOI is "unlevered" — it doesn't care how the deal is financed. That's what makes it the universal comparison metric.

The Yield Metrics

Cap Rate (Capitalization Rate)
Annual NOI divided by purchase price. The "yield" of the property if you paid all cash. Lower cap = higher price = "safer" market. Higher cap = cheaper = more risk or upside.
$1.9M NOI / $38M price = 5.0% cap
Basis Points (bps)
1/100th of a percent. Used constantly when discussing cap rate changes and spreads. "Cap rates compressed 50 bps" = they dropped 0.50%.
100 bps = 1%  |  25 bps = 0.25%
Cash-on-Cash Return
Annual pre-tax cash flow divided by total equity invested. Unlike cap rate, this factors in leverage (debt). The return you actually feel in your bank account.
$350K cash flow / $5M equity = 7.0% CoC
IRR — Internal Rate of Return
The time-weighted return across the entire hold period, including cash flow AND sale proceeds. The "gold standard" return metric for investors.
Typical value-add target: 15-20% IRR

The Financing Terms

LTV — Loan-to-Value
Loan amount divided by property value. Lenders typically cap at 65-80% LTV. Higher LTV = more leverage = more risk.
$28M loan / $38M value = 73.7% LTV
Debt Service
Total annual mortgage payments (principal + interest). This is what NOI has to cover before equity holders see a dime.
DSCR — Debt Service Coverage Ratio
NOI divided by annual debt service. Lenders require 1.20-1.35x minimum. Below 1.0x = the property can't cover its mortgage.
$1.9M NOI / $1.4M debt svc = 1.36x DSCR ✓
Equity Multiple
Total distributions / total equity invested. A 2.0x multiple means you doubled your money over the hold period.
$10M returned / $5M invested = 2.0x

Time References

T-12
Trailing 12 months of actual financial performance. The most commonly referenced historical period. "What does the T-12 show?" = "What did this property actually earn?"
T-3
Trailing 3 months, annualized. Used to see recent momentum — is performance improving or declining vs the full T-12?
PPU / PPSF
Per-unit and per-square-foot normalization. Lets you compare a 50-unit building to a 300-unit building on equal footing. "$150K per unit" and "$180 per square foot" are typical deal-size expressions.
Pro Forma
Projected future performance based on assumptions. The business plan in numbers. Buyers use pro forma to justify purchase price; sellers use T-12 to prove actual performance.

Module 2: The Property

Anatomy of a multifamily asset — what you're looking at and why it matters.

Property Classes

Multifamily properties are classified A through D based on quality, age, location, and amenities. This isn't an official rating — it's market convention, but everyone uses it.

ClassTypical AgeFinish LevelLocationTenantsCap Rate Range
A<10 yearsLuxury finishes, smart-home, resort amenitiesPrime urban/suburbanHigh income, choice renters3.5 – 5.0%
B10-25 yearsGood condition, standard amenitiesSolid neighborhoodsMiddle income, workforce4.5 – 6.0%
C25-40+ yearsDated but functional, deferred maintenanceWorking-class areasCost-conscious renters5.5 – 7.5%
D40+ yearsSignificant deferred maintenanceDistressed areasSubsidized / low-income7.0 – 10%+

Notice the relationship: as property quality decreases, cap rates increase. Investors demand more yield for more risk. This is a fundamental principle you'll see everywhere in CRE.

Value-Add: Where the Money Is Made

The most common investment strategy in multifamily is "value-add" — buying a B or C property, investing capital to improve it, and raising rents toward the next class up. The playbook:

  1. Acquire a Class B- or C+ property at a 5.5-6.5% cap rate
  2. Renovate unit interiors ($8-15K per unit) — countertops, fixtures, flooring, appliances
  3. Upgrade common areas — lobby, fitness center, pool, landscaping
  4. Push rents $100-300/unit/month as units turn over and get renovated
  5. Improve operations — better management, utility billing, ancillary income
  6. Refinance or sell at a lower cap rate (4.5-5.5%) based on higher NOI

The magic: you increase NOI AND the market applies a lower cap rate to the improved asset. Both effects push value up.

Unit Mix

The distribution of unit types in a property matters for both revenue stability and tenant demographics.

Unit TypeTypical SFTenant ProfileTurnoverRent Premium vs Studio

More bedrooms = longer average tenancy = lower turnover costs. But studios and 1BRs often have the highest rent per square foot. Most institutional investors prefer a mix weighted toward 1BR and 2BR units.

Submarkets

A metro area (MSA) contains dozens of submarkets, each with its own supply/demand dynamics. "Dallas" is an MSA; "Uptown Dallas," "Las Colinas," and "Frisco" are submarkets. Two properties five miles apart can have completely different performance profiles because they're in different submarkets.

Module 3: The Money

How multifamily deals get valued — and why cap rates rule everything around you.

The Income Approach

Unlike residential real estate (where you compare sale prices of similar homes), commercial real estate is valued based on income. The property is a cash-flow machine, and the price is a function of how much cash it produces.

Value = NOI ÷ Cap Rate

This simple formula drives billions of dollars in transactions. Two knobs control value: NOI (what the property earns) and cap rate (what yield the market demands).

Why this formula is so powerful

Say you buy a property with $1M NOI at a 6.0% cap rate. The value is $16.67M. Over two years you increase NOI to $1.2M through renovations. If the market cap rate compresses to 5.5%, the new value is:

$1.2M ÷ 0.055 = $21.82M

That's a $5.15M value increase — a 31% gain — from a 20% NOI increase plus 50 bps of cap rate compression. This is the value-add math investors obsess over.

Cap Rate Spreads

Cap rates don't exist in a vacuum — they're always discussed relative to the risk-free rate, typically the 10-Year US Treasury yield. The "spread" is the gap between them.

Scenario10Y TreasuryCap RateSpreadSignal
Normal4.0%5.5%150 bpsHealthy risk premium
Tight4.0%4.5%50 bpsPossible overheating — buyers accepting thin premium
Wide4.0%7.0%300 bpsDistress or high perceived risk — potential opportunity

In conversations, you might hear: "Spreads are too tight for us to underwrite — we need at least 150 over the 10-year to pencil." This means the buyer won't pay a price that puts the cap rate less than 150 bps above Treasuries.

Comparable Sales

While the income approach is primary, it's validated by looking at what similar properties ("comps") actually sold for. Comps are normalized on a per-unit (PPU) or per-square-foot (PPSF) basis.

Good comp criteria: same submarket, similar class/vintage, within 12-18 months, similar unit count range. "I see three Class B comps in this submarket at $125-140K per door" means recent B properties sold for $125K-$140K per unit.

Module 4: The Rent Roll

The revenue engine — how to read the most important document in any deal.

What Is a Rent Roll?

A rent roll is a snapshot of every unit in a property: who's in it, what they pay, and when their lease ends. It's the source of truth for current revenue and the starting point for underwriting future performance.

Key Concepts When Reading Rent Rolls

Loss-to-Lease (LTL)
The difference between market rent and in-place rent, summed across all units. Shows how much revenue you're "leaving on the table." High LTL = organic rent growth opportunity.
Mkt $1,450 - In-place $1,350 = $100 LTL on Unit 101
Mark-to-Market
The analysis of bringing every lease to current market rent. If total mark-to-market is +8%, that means in-place rents are 8% below market — significant upside at renewal.
Physical Occupancy
Occupied units ÷ total units. Simple headcount. Above: 3 occupied + 1 notice / 5 total = 60-80% depending on how you count notice.
Economic Occupancy
Actual collected rent ÷ gross potential rent. Always lower than physical occupancy because it captures concessions, bad debt, and vacancy. This is the number that matters for underwriting.
Lease Expiration Schedule
When leases roll — you want them staggered, not all expiring the same month. Concentrated expirations in winter months = higher vacancy risk.
Renewal Rate
Percentage of expiring leases that renew. Typical stabilized: 50-60%. Renewals are cheaper than new leases (no turnover cost, no downtime, lower commission).

Module 5: The Deal

Lifecycle of a multifamily acquisition — from first look to exit.

Deal Stages

StageDurationWhat Happens
1. SourcingOngoingIdentify deals through broker relationships, off-market networks, auction platforms. Screen against investment criteria (market, size, class, return targets).
2. Underwriting1-2 weeksBuild a pro forma: model income, expenses, renovations, financing, and projected returns. Decide on offer price.
3. LOI3-7 daysLetter of Intent — non-binding offer with key terms: price, deposit, due diligence period, closing timeline. Seller may counter.
4. PSA1-2 weeksPurchase & Sale Agreement — binding contract. Defines reps/warranties, contingencies, and earnest money (usually 1-3% of price).
5. Due Diligence30-60 daysDeep dive: physical inspections, environmental (Phase I/II), title search, lease audit, financial verification, insurance review. This is where deals die or get re-traded.
6. Financing45-60 daysSecure debt: agency (Fannie/Freddie), bridge loan, bank loan, or CMBS. Lender does their own appraisal and underwriting.
7. Closing1 dayWire funds, transfer deed, record documents. Property changes hands.

Total timeline from LOI to close: typically 60-90 days for conventional deals, 30-45 for bridge/all-cash.

The Capital Stack

How a deal gets funded — who puts in money and who gets paid first.

GP Equity (5-20%)
Highest risk, highest return. The operators.
LP Equity (15-30%)
Limited Partners — passive investors. Get preferred return + profit split.
Mezzanine / Pref Equity (0-15%)
Optional middle layer. Higher cost than senior debt, lower priority than equity.
Senior Debt (60-75%)
First priority — gets paid first. Lowest cost, lowest risk.

↑ Higher risk / return     ↓ Lower risk / return. Payments flow bottom-up: debt first, then mezz, then LP, then GP.

Key Financing Types

Agency Debt (Fannie Mae / Freddie Mac)
Government-backed loans for stabilized multifamily (90%+ occupancy). Best rates, longest terms (7-12 years), but strict requirements. The gold standard.
Bridge Loan
Short-term (1-3 years) floating-rate loan for transitional assets. Higher rate but allows interest-only payments during renovation. The value-add workhorse.
CMBS
Commercial Mortgage-Backed Securities — loans bundled and sold to bond investors. Competitive rates but inflexible terms and difficult to modify. "Securitized" debt.
GP / LP Structure
GP (General Partner) operates the deal and earns fees + promote. LP (Limited Partner) contributes capital passively. Typical split: LP gets 8% preferred return, then profits split 70/30 or 80/20 (LP/GP).

Module 6: The Market

How to read, analyze, and sound smart about multifamily markets.

The Big Four Indicators

Every market conversation comes down to four forces. Master these and you can discuss any market intelligently.

1. Absorption
Net new units occupied over a period. Positive absorption = demand outpacing supply. The primary demand signal. "Dallas absorbed 12,000 units in the last 12 months" = strong demand.
2. Deliveries (New Supply)
Units completed and available for occupancy. When deliveries exceed absorption, occupancy drops and rents soften. "Phoenix has 25,000 units delivering this year" = supply headwind.
3. Occupancy
The equilibrium between supply and demand. 95%+ = "tight" market favoring landlords. Below 92% = softening. Below 88% = distress. Watch the trend, not just the number.
4. Rent Growth
Year-over-year change in effective rents. The lagging indicator — rent growth follows occupancy trends with a 2-6 month delay. Negative rent growth signals oversupply.

Supply-Demand Framework

ScenarioAbsorption vs DeliveriesOccupancy TrendRent GrowthPosture
BullishAbsorption > DeliveriesRising / Stable >95%Positive 3-5%+Push rents, invest
NeutralRoughly balancedStable 93-95%Flat to low single digitsHold, selective invest
BearishDeliveries > AbsorptionFalling below 93%Negative or flatConcede on rents, wait

Pipeline & Forward Supply

Deliveries tell you what's happening now. The construction pipeline tells you what's coming. In multifamily, it takes 18-24 months from groundbreaking to delivery. So today's "under construction" number = next year's supply.

When someone says "Nashville has 3 years of pipeline," they mean that at the current pace of absorption, it would take 3 years to absorb all planned and under-construction units. That's a lot of supply.

Reading the Signals

Concession Trends
Rising concessions = softening market. Landlords are competing for tenants. Watch for "6-8 weeks free" — that signals meaningful oversupply.
Permit Activity
Leading indicator of future supply. High permits today = deliveries in 18-24 months. If permits are dropping, the supply pipeline is slowing — potentially bullish for existing owners.
Job Growth
The fundamental driver of housing demand. Markets with strong, diversified employment bases absorb supply more easily. Tech-heavy markets can swing sharply with layoff cycles.
Migration Patterns
Domestic in-migration drives demand in Sun Belt markets. Remote work has amplified this — people moving from high-cost coastal metros to lower-cost Sun Belt cities.

Conversation Phrases You Should Know

  • "The market is in a supply trough" — Few new units coming online. Good for existing owners — less competition.
  • "Cap rates are widening in secondary markets" — Buyers are demanding higher yields (lower prices) in smaller cities. Risk premium increasing.
  • "We're seeing negative leverage" — The cap rate is below the interest rate on debt. Dangerous: you're paying more for debt than the property yields.
  • "Rent growth is reverting to mean" — After an abnormal period (COVID-era spikes), growth is returning to the long-term average (2-4% annually).
  • "The bid-ask spread is still wide" — Sellers want higher prices than buyers will pay. Deals aren't getting done. Transaction volume is low.
  • "We're penciling a 5-year exit at a 25 bps compression" — Their financial model assumes cap rates will be 0.25% lower when they sell in 5 years.
